Having a website doesn't guarantee sales. Plenty of businesses have a good-looking site that **doesn't produce a single lead**, and the owner concludes that "the internet doesn't work for my industry." It's almost never the industry. The site is missing pieces you can't see at a glance.

This is the list I use to review any service business website. Each point comes with a test you can run yourself today, with no technical knowledge.

## 1. A clear message in the first five seconds

When someone lands, they need to understand **what you do, for whom and where** without scrolling. If they have to guess, they leave and don't come back.

The typical mistake is an aspirational headline: "Comprehensive solutions for your growth," "Quality and commitment." They say nothing. Compare that with "Kitchen and bathroom remodeling in Houston, quotes within 48 hours." The second one pulls in the right visitor and filters out the wrong one, which is also useful.

**The test:** show your homepage to someone who doesn't know your business for five seconds, then take it away. Ask what you sell and in which city. If they can't answer both, rewrite the headline.

## 2. One main action, visible in every section

Every section should invite a specific action: "Get a quote," "Book," "Text us." And it should be **the same action** across the site. Three different buttons competing ("Learn more," "Our services," "Contact us") split attention, and none of them wins.

Pick one main action and, at most, one secondary. For a business that quotes jobs, the main one is usually a call, text or form; for a practice, it's booking.

**The test:** scroll your page on a phone. Is there any stretch of two full screens without a button in sight? That's where people slip away.

## 3. Load speed

A page that takes too long loses visitors before they see what you offer, especially on phones and mobile data. Speed isn't a technical detail; it's the first impression.

The causes are almost always the same: photos uploaded straight from the camera (four or five megabytes each), themes loaded with animations and unused plugins, and cheap overloaded hosting.

**The test:** open your site on your phone with Wi-Fi off. If you count more than three seconds before you see content, there's work to do. Google's PageSpeed Insights tells you what's slowing it down.

## 4. Designed for the phone first

Most of your visitors arrive on a phone. "Responsive" doesn't just mean the layout adjusts; it means the site can be **used with a thumb**: big buttons, text readable without zooming, a phone number that dials when tapped, and forms that don't ask for ten fields on a tiny keyboard. More in [what responsive web design really means](/en/blog/responsive-web-design/).

**The test:** try to contact yourself through your site, on your phone, using one hand. Any step that needed both hands needs redesigning.

## 5. Proof people can verify

People trust people, but they distrust anything generic. "500+ happy customers" or "The best quality in town" convince no one. What does lower the doubt:

- Photos of your real work, with place and date.
- Reviews with full names, ideally linked to Google.
- Your face and your name: in services, people hire people.
- License, insurance or certifications, if your trade has them.
- Projects people can actually visit, like the ones in the [portfolio](/en/portfolio/).

**The test:** count the verifiable elements on your homepage. Fewer than three, and visitors are trusting you on faith.

## 6. Frictionless contact

The easier it is to reach you, the more inquiries you get. What works best for a service business:

- Text or WhatsApp with a pre-written message, so the customer just hits send.
- Phone number in the header, not buried in the footer.
- A three-field form: name, phone and what they need. Everything else can be asked later.
- Online booking if you sell appointments.

Every extra form field cuts submissions. Address, budget and email can wait until you're already talking.

**The test:** fill out your own form. Did the notification reach your email or phone within a minute? It's surprising how many forms have been sending leads to an inbox nobody checks for months.

## 7. A structure that guides, not just informs

A good page walks the visitor through **problem → solution → proof → action.** It's not a brochure of everything you do; it's a path to "yes."

In practice the order matters: first the problem the customer recognizes ("your kitchen is 20 years old"), then how you solve it, then evidence you've done it before, and finally what they need to do to start. It also means **one page per service**: someone searching for roofing doesn't want to read about plumbing.

**The test:** read your homepage top to bottom as a customer with a specific problem. Did you reach the right service page in one click? Were you told the next step at the end?

## What to do with the result

If your site fails one or two points, it's an afternoon of fixes. If it fails four or more, it's usually cheaper to rebuild with the right structure than to patch the current theme.
