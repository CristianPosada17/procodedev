The question is usually framed as two clean categories, and they aren't. There are **web design agencies** of two people and **freelance web designers** with more process than a mid-size agency. What really changes between the options comes down to four things: price, timelines, risk, and what happens after launch.

## Price

| | Freelancer | Small agency | Large agency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Service business website | $200–$1,500 | $350–$3,000 | $3,000–$15,000 |
| What explains the gap | No overhead | A small team and processes | Account managers, management, offices |

A higher price doesn't guarantee better work, but a very low price does guarantee something: someone will cut hours from the part you can't see (structure, speed, technical SEO, tracking), because it's the only way the numbers work.

## Timelines

A freelancer usually starts sooner because there's no project queue. An agency more often **finishes**, because there's a process and someone watching the calendar.

The typical risk on each side: the freelancer disappears for two weeks when a bigger project comes in; the large agency makes you wait three weeks for a text change because it has to pass through three people.

## Risk

This is the most important difference, and the one almost nobody evaluates.

With a freelancer, **the risk is continuity:** if they get sick, take a job or simply stop replying, your project stops and sometimes gets lost. With an agency, the risk is **attention:** you're one client among forty, and if you're not one of the big ones, your project waits in line.

How to lower the freelancer risk without giving up the price:

- Domain and hosting **in your name**, with your logins, from day one. - Payment in stages, tied to deliverables, not everything upfront.
- Code stored in a repository you can access.
- Ask directly: "What happens if something happens to you, or you're unavailable for six weeks?"

## What happens after launch

This is where most projects fall apart. The site goes live, and eight months later a service needs changing, a certificate expires, or the form stops sending emails.

Ask either option before signing: **do you offer maintenance, what does it cost, and what does it include?** If the answer is "just message me anytime," there's no agreement, only goodwill, and goodwill runs out.

## When each one makes sense

**A freelancer**, if:

- Your project is one to six pages and well defined.
- Your budget is under $1,000.
- You want to talk directly with the person doing the work.
- You can keep the accounts in your name and pay in stages.

**A small agency**, if:

- You want someone who'll answer tomorrow and next year.
- You need the website and lead generation coordinated, not two vendors blaming each other.
- Process, backups and maintenance matter to you.

**A large agency**, if:

- Several people on your side make decisions.
- You need compliance, formal contracts and corporate invoicing.
- The budget is in five figures or more.

## How to choose, in practice

Ask two or three providers for the same thing and compare these five answers:

1. Exactly what the price includes: design, development, technical SEO, tracking, launch?
2. Whose name the domain and hosting are in.
3. How many rounds of revisions are included.
4. What they need from you, and by when.
5. What happens with maintenance afterward.

Whoever answers all five with numbers and no hedging is the safe bet, agency or freelancer. Whoever dodges #2 and #5 isn't. For the factors behind the price itself, see [how much a small business website costs](/en/blog/small-business-website-cost/).
