**Website architecture** is how content is split across pages and how those pages connect. It's the cheapest decision to make and the most expensive to fix: changing colors takes an afternoon; changing the **structure of a website** that's already indexed means redirects, lost rankings and months of waiting.

## The rule that organizes everything: one intent, one page

Every page should answer **one** thing someone searches for. A page that tries to answer three wins none: not on Google, which can't tell what it's about, and not with the visitor, who has to filter out what doesn't apply.

In practice, for a service business:

```
Home                        → who you are and what you do
├── Services                → the map of what you sell
│   ├── Service A           → a complete page
│   ├── Service B
│   └── Service C
├── Industries              → if you serve different industries
│   └── One page per industry
├── Portfolio               → the proof
├── Pricing                 → the most searched answer
├── Blog                    → attracts people not ready to buy yet
└── Contact
```

Three levels at most. If a page is four clicks from the homepage, Google gives it little weight and visitors never find it.

## The menu isn't an index of everything you have

An eleven-item menu forces eleven decisions. Five or six, with the rest in dropdowns or the footer.

Order matters too: first and last items are remembered most. "Contact" at the far right isn't tradition; it's where the hand looks for it.

## Internal linking: the part almost nobody does

Menu and footer links appear on every page, so Google gives them little weight. The links that count are **inside the content**, with anchor text that says what's on the other side.

Three rules that work:

- A service page links to pricing and to a real case of that service.
- Every blog post links to the commercial page that solves what the post explains.
- A hub page links to all its children, and each child links back.

Write "see website pricing," not "click here": anchor text tells Google what the destination page is about.

## UX: five rules that move the needle

1. **Five seconds.** On arrival, people should understand what you do, for whom and where, without scrolling.
2. **One main action per page**, repeated three times: top, middle and end.
3. **The thumb rules.** Important buttons go in the lower half of a phone screen.
4. **Fewer fields, more submissions.** Three fields convert much better than seven.
5. **No dead ends.** Every page ends by offering the next step.

## What breaks architecture without you noticing

- **Cannibalization:** two pages competing for the same search. Google picks one, rarely the one you wanted. Fix it by deciding which phrase belongs to which page and rewriting the other.
- **Orphan pages:** published but with no internal links. Nobody arrives, and Google barely considers them.
- **A disconnected blog:** posts that get visits but lead to no commercial page. Traffic that does nothing for the business.

## How to review it in twenty minutes

Sketch your site on paper: a box per page, an arrow for every internal link inside the content. You'll see three things immediately: which pages get no arrows, which pages say the same thing, and how many clicks separate the homepage from your best-selling page.
