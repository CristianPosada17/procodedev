**What is web design**, in short: deciding how a site looks and how it's used so the visitor does what they came to do. It's not drawing a pretty page; it's organizing information so someone in a hurry understands and acts.

## Design and development aren't the same

This is the confusion that costs the most money:

- **Design** = how it looks and how people move through it. Structure, hierarchy, colors, type, where each button goes.
- **Development** = turning that into a working site, with code.

Plenty of people hire "web design" and receive a nice image nobody can visit. When you ask for a quote, ask explicitly whether it includes building and launching. A complete **web design and development** project includes both.

## The parts of a real project

**1. Strategy.** Who you sell to, what that person searches on Google, and what you want them to do. Without this, everything else is decoration.

**2. Architecture.** Which pages exist and how they connect. One page per service, not a list. More in [website architecture and UX](/en/blog/website-architecture-ux/).

**3. Content.** Text and photos. The part that delays projects most, and improves them most when done well.

**4. Visual design.** Colors, type, spacing, the final look. This is where mockups come in.

**5. Development.** The code. This decides whether the site loads in one second or six.

**6. Technical SEO.** Titles, descriptions, structured data, sitemap, speed. It should be included; ask.

**7. Tracking.** Analytics and events so you know how many leads came from the site.

**8. Launch.** Domain, hosting, email, security certificate, all in your name. ## What makes web design good

Not a design award. Four measurable things:

- **It's understood in five seconds:** what you do, for whom and where.
- **It loads in under three seconds** on a phone with mobile data.
- **It works with a thumb,** no zooming or sideways scrolling.
- **It ends in a clear action,** repeated and easy to tap.

An ugly site that meets all four outsells a beautiful one that fails two.

## Questions to ask before hiring

1. Does it include development and launch, or only design?
2. Are the domain and hosting in my name?
3. Can I edit text and photos myself afterward?
4. If I leave someday, do I take the site with me?
5. Is technical SEO included or extra?
6. How many revision rounds are included?
7. What do you need from me, and by when?

The answers to #2 and #4 separate a vendor from a hostage situation: if the domain is in the agency's name, the site isn't fully yours. For choosing between providers, see [web design agency vs. freelancer](/en/blog/web-design-agency-vs-freelancer/).

## What it costs and why it varies so much

The range you see, $200 to $10,000, is real, and it's not that some charge more for fun. The difference is whether the price covers all eight steps or just two, whether the site is custom or a filled-in template, and how many pages there are.

For an established service business, the reasonable range is $350 to $1,500 one-time. It's all broken down on [the pricing page](/en/pricing/), and the factors that move the number are in [how much a small business website costs](/en/blog/small-business-website-cost/).

## If you're starting from zero

The order I recommend: first decide what job the site will do, then gather content (the ten questions customers ask most and your real photos), and only then choose a platform or provider. The other way around, you pick the tool before you know what it's for.
