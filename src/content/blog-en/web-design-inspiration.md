Most people look for **web design inspiration** in award galleries. The problem is that those galleries reward risk, not sales: agency sites built to impress other agencies. Copying that for a service business is the most elegant way to lose customers.

Here's the other way to look at references: by **design style**, with the type of business each one works for.

## 1. Minimalist

Lots of white space, little text, one idea per screen. It works when **the product or the work speaks for itself**: photography, architecture, interior design, beautiful products.

When it fails: when you sell a service people don't fully understand. Minimalism removes exactly the text needed to convince them.

## 2. Classic corporate

Blues, predictable structure, many sections. Boring to a designer, effective for an accounting firm or a builder that needs to look stable.

Predictability is a feature, not a flaw: visitors know where everything is because they've seen it on ten similar sites.

## 3. Dark and technical

Black background, color accents, monospaced type. It works for software, security, technical services and anything sold to people who work in front of a screen.

When it fails: for businesses whose customers are over 55. Low contrast on dark backgrounds is hard on many eyes, and half of dark sites get it wrong.

## 4. Editorial

Large type, long-form text, magazine rhythm. Ideal for [consultants and professional services](/en/professional-services/), [attorneys](/en/professional-services/#attorneys) and anyone selling **judgment**: what you buy is how that person thinks, and that's shown through writing.

## 5. Brutalism

Hard edges, flat colors, huge type. It grabs attention and ages fast. It suits brands selling personality (creative studios, youth fashion) and is an expensive risk anywhere else.

## 6. Cards and blocks

Everything lives in cards: services, case studies, prices. It's the most common style for service businesses for a simple reason: it adapts to phones without a fight and lets people scan the page in ten seconds.

## 7. Full-width photo

A real image filling the screen with the message on top. Very powerful **if the photo is yours.** With a stock photo the opposite happens: visitors recognize the trick and trust drops.

## 8. One long page

The whole business in a single scroll. Good for a single offer or a campaign. Bad for ranking on Google: one page ranks for very few things. More in [landing page vs. website](/en/blog/landing-page-vs-website/).

## What gets copied wrong

When someone sends me three references, they've almost always copied the same things:

- **The animation, not the hierarchy.** What makes that site good is that you know what to look at first, not that things fade in as you scroll.
- **A famous brand's hero.** Nike can show one photo and one word because you already know what it sells. You're not there yet.
- **The twelve-item menu.** They have it because they have twelve lines of business.
- **A very long form.** If your competitors ask for nine fields, ask for three and you've already won.

## How to use references without copying them

When you see a site you like, write three sentences: what I understood in the first five seconds, what they want me to do, and what made me trust them. Those three answers transfer to your business. Colors and animations, not so much.
