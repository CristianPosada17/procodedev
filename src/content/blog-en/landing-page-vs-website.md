One of the first questions when a business goes online: **do I need a landing page or a full website?** Both work, but not for the same thing, and choosing wrong is expensive in two ways: you pay for pages nobody visits, or you send ad traffic to a site where customers get lost.

## What a landing page is

A landing page is **one page with one goal**: get the visitor to take a specific action. Leave their details, call, text, book or buy. No long menu, no sections that distract; everything on the page pushes toward that action.

A good landing page has, in this order: a headline that says exactly what you offer, the problem it solves, how it works, proof (reviews, photos, cases), common questions, and the button, repeated several times.

It's ideal when:

- You're launching **a specific service or promotion** and want to measure response.
- You're running **ads** on Google or Meta and need a destination that converts. Sending paid traffic to your homepage is one of the most expensive mistakes I see.
- You're just starting and need a **professional presence this week** on a tight budget.
- You sell **one main service** and everything else is secondary.

## What a website is

A website has **several pages**: home, one per service, about, projects or case studies, contact and, if it makes sense, a blog. It tells your business's full story, answers the questions of people comparing options, and gives Google material to find you.

It's ideal when:

- You have **several services**, each searched with different words.
- You want to be found on **Google** without paying per click. A one-page landing rarely ranks for more than one search.
- Your customer **compares before hiring**: attorneys, medical practices, contractors on larger projects.
- You serve **several cities or two languages**, which in practice means more pages.

## The difference in one sentence

A landing page is **a salesperson focused on one offer.** A website is **your whole business online**, serving people who arrive with different questions.

## Examples by type of business

- **A contractor just starting Facebook ads** for bathroom remodels: landing page. One offer, one action (request a quote) and a clear way to measure leads per dollar.
- **An accounting firm with six services** (individual taxes, business taxes, payroll, bookkeeping, ITIN, audits): website. Each service is searched separately.
- **A nutritionist selling online consultations:** can start with a landing page with built-in booking and grow into a website when they want to publish content and appear in local searches.
- **A business with a working website launching a seasonal promotion:** both. The website keeps doing its job and the landing page takes the campaign traffic.

## Which one do you need?

Three questions settle most cases:

1. **Where will people come from?** Ads or a link you share: landing page. Google search: website.
2. **How many distinct services do you sell?** One: landing page. Three or more: website.
3. **How much does your customer compare before deciding?** Quick decisions (an emergency, an offer): landing page. Days of research: website.

If the answers are mixed, the most common route is **start with a landing page and grow into a website.** Done right, the landing page later becomes that service's page inside the site, and nothing you invested is lost.

## The most common mistakes

- **A landing page with a full menu.** Links to the whole site make it stop being a landing page: the visitor wanders off and doesn't take the action.
- **A website with a single "Services" page.** Five services in one list are five searches you won't win.
- **Ads pointing to the homepage.** The homepage talks about everything; the ad promised one thing. The customer can't find what was offered and leaves.
